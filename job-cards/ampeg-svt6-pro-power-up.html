Standing hazards — read before every session

  • The output is not ground-referenced. This PCB is hardwired mono-bridge. Never put a grounded scope probe on either output leg — use differential, isolated or floating measurement.
  • Filter caps hold lethal charge long after power-down. Six 5600 µF/80 V plus two 47 µF/450 V (C14, C15) on a ~296 V B+. Discharge and verify 0 V before touching anything.
  • A variac is an autotransformer, not an isolator. Always feed it from an isolation transformer.
  • Mains-potential taps. The unused transformer primary tap sits at line potential when powered. It must be insulated, never landed on a tab.
A

Before power

power off
07

Set AP1 and AP2 to minimum bias.

Both are 10K trimpots, marked AP1 and AP2 on the print — not R26 or R100. AP1 is topside next to Q4 (plus half); AP2 is on the underside, through the access hole (minus half). R26 and R100 are fixed resistors — don't go near them.

Anticlockwise is minimum. Wind both fully anticlockwise, amp off, before you ramp. Traced off the print: the pot sits base-to-emitter on the bias transistor, so full 10K is the smallest spread.

Single-turn pots — the whole bias range is inside about 270°, and it's badly non-linear. The first half of the travel does almost nothing, then it comes up fast, and the last of it is vicious. Expect to finish roughly two thirds back toward clockwise. Work in small fractions and let the meter settle.

At the 120 V pause on the ramp, read the millivolts before you touch either pot. Near zero is expected. 30 mV+ means something is wrong — variac straight down and find out. Then give one pot a quarter turn just to see how far it moves, and put it back.

Variac down first, trimmer second. Both directions, no exceptions. Twenty IRFP240/9240 at an unknown idle current is the one load in this amp that can run away.

B

Variac ramp

no current limiting — hand on the dial
14

Isolation transformer → variac → amp. Never variac alone.

15

Clip meters on before you dial anything up: one source resistor per bank, one main rail, ammeter in line where you can see it without looking away from the variac.

With two meters, cover it in this order:
Meter A — DC millivolts across R32 (0.47 Ω 5 W, bank 1). Probes on the resistor's two ends, not either end to ground. 20 mV = 42 mA through that FET. This is the bias reading and the runaway warning.
Meter B — DC volts, +65 V rail to chassis ground. Shows the rails tracking the mains and tells you when you're near the 150–180 V relay click.
Current — use the variac's own panel ammeter, or a clamp meter on the mains lead. Don't tie up a DMM on it; during a ramp you need both hands and both eyes elsewhere.
Bank 2 (R85) — move Meter A over once bank 1 reads sane. You don't need both banks live at once during the ramp.

Both ends of a source resistor float at high voltage. A DMM is fine floating like that. A scope is not — its ground lead is bonded to mains earth, so clipping it across a source resistor shorts a rail to ground. Same reason step 21 says measure across the two output legs and never leg-to-ground.

16

Ramp in steps, pause 30 s at each and log the reading.

At the 120 V pause, stop and do the trimmer check in step 7 — read the bias millivolts before touching either pot, confirm you wound to the minimum end and not the maximum one, and take the quarter-turn feel reading. Don't go past 120 V until that's settled.

Ramp log
MainsExpectedActual IRail VObservations
30–50 V Tens of mA. Over ~200 mA — stop. That's a short.
50–100 V Gentle rise. Tube heaters warming.
150–180 V Relays should click in. D11 is a 30 V zener — UVLO releases here.
230 V Idle roughly 0.5–1 A once biased.

Relays click in at 150–180 V on the variac — and give it 1–3 seconds after you get there before you call it dead. The delay is C12 (2.2 µF) charging through D11 into IC1-D — so the click is delayed, not instant. (C11, the 220 µF reservoir, sits straight off +65 V through D10 and fills almost instantly; it isn't what times the delay.) Below about 120 V it will never release: the +65 V rail scales with the mains, and D11 is a 30 V zener that has to be passed before the circuit lets go.

If they pull in at 150–180 V, the ±16 V rail, IC1, the under-voltage chain and the relay drivers are all good. If they haven't clicked by 200 V, stop there — don't push on to 230. Variac down and find out why.

17

Abort criteria — know these before you start.

Kill it immediately if either occurs:
  • Current climbing while voltage is held constant — that's thermal runaway building.
  • Source-resistor millivolts drifting upward on their own.
Variac down first. Trimmer second. Never the other way round.

Conditions: cold start, no load, Tube Voltage control at '300V'.

19

AP1 → average 20 ±5 mVdc across bank 1.

Millivolts, across one source resistor at a time — never volts. 20 mV across 0.47 Ω is 42 mA through that FET. 20 V across it would be 42 A, and the bank would be gone before you looked up. Any reading in whole volts across a source resistor means variac straight down. The few-volt figures in step 7 are internal to the bias spreader and are never something you dial to.

21

Run 15–20 min, then re-check both banks.

IRFP240/9240 are positive-tempco at low idle. Bias will climb as the sinks warm — expect to pull it back. A single resistor is a proxy, not the spec; the spec is the average of ten.

D

Before handover

21b

Swap the speaker-line fuse at J31 for a 15 A slow-blow.

It only protects the cab, not the FETs. Keep a fresh 4 A slow-blow in for any bench load work — the one the amp arrived with is blown. 15 A decided 13 Oct: full power into 4 Ω is 16.6 A, but Pete won't get near that.

22

Fit the correct 8 A breaker. Currently 7 A.

Print calls out 15 A domestic/Japan, 8 A export. 7 A is undersized 12.5% — the safe direction, but marginal on inrush (six 5600 µF plus a large EI transformer) and at sustained high output. Never go above 8 A — that's the transformer's protection.

23

Confirm the ±16 V rail still holds ≥15 V under full load, and that the resistor and zener standoff work is secure.

The resistors are fine as stock — don't uprate them. The heat is the zeners: they dump whatever the load leaves over, up to 1.8 W, through leads only. Standing them off is the repair. If the measured load came in over ~90 mA, the rail will sag and the real fix is a TO-220 series-pass transistor using the zener as its reference — quote that separately, don't do it inside this job.

23b

Measure DC offset across the speaker output, no load, warm. Should be a few tens of millivolts at most.

Measure across the two output legs, not from either leg to chassis ground. The output is bridged and neither side sits at ground. A big offset here means one half is biased differently from the other — find that before a speaker does.

23c

Soak test, quiet. Only after the offset check passes. Two 8 Ω 100 W resistors in parallel = 4 Ω, 200 W across the output, fresh 4 A slow-blow in J31. Steady tone (100 Hz or 1 kHz) into the input, turned up until the load is warm. Run 1–2 hours.

Both output leads go to the load — neither to ground. Keep it gentle: the 4 A fuse opens around 64 W, and that's plenty for a soak.

Every 15–20 min: heatsinks warm not scorching, no smell, relays still in, current steady.
At the end: re-check bias on both banks while warm, then a brief low-volume listen on a speaker to confirm it's clean.

24

Closed — nothing to ask. This step wanted the fuse’s history from the customer.

Pete bought the amp second-hand in this condition and has no history to give. The best account is the plain one: the previous tech fitted a fuse roughly 4× undersized, it blew on a loud night with nothing failing downstream, and the amp went back still dead. No hidden intermittent needs inventing to explain it.
